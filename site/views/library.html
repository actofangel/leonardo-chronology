The Library of Leonardo

omo sanza lettere

"a man without letters" · written here in mirror script, as Leonardo wrote

Leonardo da Vinci called himself an omo sanza lettere, a man without letters. His manuscripts tell another story. By the end of his life he owned nearly two hundred books, an extraordinary number for an artist and engineer of the fifteenth century. This site reconstructs that lost library: the 125 titles that can be identified today from the book lists, quotations, and notes scattered through his own hand.

The Myth

Unmaking the "man without letters"

The phrase omo sanza lettere long fed a legend: a solitary genius who learned nothing from books and everything from nature. Modern bibliography has dismantled it. Leonardo grew up inside a culture of writing. His father, Ser Piero, was a notary; his grandfather Antonio kept the family record book that notes Leonardo's birth; his half brother Lorenzo, a wool merchant, wrote a Libro di patientia and a confessional manual quoting Dante and Petrarch. Leonardo's own handwriting is the mercantesca, the merchant's hand of Florentine tradesmen and clerks.

What he lacked was Latin, the language of the universities, and much of his library documents a lifelong campaign to conquer it: grammars of Donatus and Perotti, model letter collections, dictionaries, and a word list of more than eight thousand entries that he compiled himself, his libro di mia vocaboli. The self description was less confession than provocation. Book learning, he argued, must answer to experience; the authors he called altori were partners in an argument he intended to win by observation. The library below is the record of that argument.

Primary Evidence

Three lists in his own hand

The reconstruction rests on inventories Leonardo himself jotted into his notebooks at three moments of his life. Read in sequence, they chart the growth of a working library from five schoolroom titles to a private collection rivaling a scholar's.

Milan · c. 1487 to 1490

Codex Trivulzianus

Cod. Triv. 2162, f. 2r 5 titles

The earliest record: Donatus, a lapidary, Pliny, an abacus book, and Pulci's Morgante. The same codex holds his vast word lists, over 8,000 entries drawn from Pulci, Valturio, Masuccio, and Poggio, including whole clusters of terms for turbulence, whirlwind, and storm that would later power his descriptions of water and air.

Milan · c. 1495

Codex Atlanticus

f. 559r · written in red chalk ~40 titles

A rapidly expanding shelf, still dominated by literature, grammar, and rhetoric, but with scientific and technical works beginning to appear. Books are now instruments of dialogue with the altori, the authors ancient and modern.

Florence · 1503 to 1504

Madrid Codex II

Cod. Madrid 8936, ff. 2v to 3r 116 titles

The mature library: philosophy, geometry, astronomy, cosmography, medicine, and anatomy, much of it now in Latin. Leonardo notes that 98 volumes are locked away serati nel cassone, in the great chest, with the rest stored in a second box.

The Catalogue

Every identifiable title, ordered as Leonardo's own entries suggest and grouped by subject. Influence

Reading Leonardo

The catalogue is not a list of dead titles. Traced through the notebooks, these books show how Leonardo absorbed the knowledge of his age and turned it into something new.

Natural Philosophy

Pliny, water, and the moon

Leonardo owned the Natural History of Pliny the Elder in Cristoforo Landino's Italian translation of 1476, and read it hard. In the Codex Leicester, drafted around 1508 to 1510, Pliny is the constant point of reference when Leonardo argues about the light of the moon and the movement of the earth's waters, treating the dark lunar maria as seas like our own and testing the ancient authority against his own eyes. Mandeville's book of marvels, Foresti's world chronicle, and Ptolemy's cosmography fed the same habit of mind: the correspondence of macrocosm and microcosm, world and body.

Mathematics

Pacioli and the discipline of geometry

The mathematician Luca Pacioli arrived in Milan in 1496 and transformed Leonardo's shelf. Under his guidance Leonardo worked through a vernacular Euclid; Madrid Codex II, f. 78r, carries a tree of proportion drawn straight out of Pacioli's Summa de arithmetica. Leonardo repaid the debt with the celebrated polyhedra he drew for Pacioli's De divina proportione, and his pursuit of proportion in painting and architecture moved from workshop rule of thumb toward exact mathematical law. Witelo, Pecham, and Giorgio Valla supplied the optics behind his theory of vision.

Medicine

Anatomy against the book

His shelves held a Latin medical booklet, a book of anatomy, Montagnana on urines, and the great surgery of Guy de Chauliac, channels for Galen, Avicenna, Celsus, and Soranus. Academic anatomy of the day was still recitation: the professor read the authority aloud while the cadaver illustrated it. Leonardo inverted the ritual. He began from the books, then cut, looked, and corrected the text with the knife, producing anatomical drawings that put direct observation above written authority and opened a door to modern science.

Painting

Fables, theology, and the painted idea

The moral bestiary Fiore di virtù and his three copies of Aesop stand directly behind the fables and emblematic animals of the notebooks, texts he turned into rebuses and allegories. Theology reached the easel too: the prophetic Apocalypsis Nova of Amadeus of Portugal, with its teaching on the Immaculate Conception and the privileged sinlessness of the infant John the Baptist, has been proposed as the doctrinal ground of both versions of the Virgin of the Rocks, down to the angel Uriel's pointing hand in the Louvre panel. Painting, for Leonardo, was a discorso mentale, a discourse of the mind fed by everything he read.

What Remains

The one that survived

Ivolume · of nearly 200

Francesco di Giorgio Martini, Trattato di architettura civile e militare

At Leonardo's death in 1519 the library passed to his beloved pupil Francesco Melzi, and in the centuries that followed it was scattered to the winds. A single physical volume that Leonardo demonstrably owned and used has come down to us: a manuscript of Francesco di Giorgio's treatise on civil and military architecture, carrying about a dozen marginal notes in Leonardo's own hand.

Everything else on this site is reconstruction: titles recovered from his lists, his quotations, and the names of authors he argued with on paper.

Digital Humanities

Rebuilding a lost library

The scholarly reconstruction behind this catalogue is above all the work of Carlo Vecce and an international team coordinated with the Museo Galileo in Florence and the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin. Their exhibition Leonardo and His Books: The Library of the Universal Genius opened in Florence in 2019 for the fifth centenary of Leonardo's death and traveled to the Berlin State Library in 2021 as Leonardo's Intellectual Cosmos, accompanied by a full catalogue published by Giunti.

The Museo Galileo's thematic digital library, La Biblioteca di Leonardo, assembles digitized pages of the specific incunabula and manuscripts Leonardo is likely to have consulted, cross referenced against his notebooks, so that a reader today can follow which page he read, which figure struck him, and where it resurfaces in his own hand. It is less a list of books than a map of a mind, and it is the destination of every link in the catalogue above.
